# Finding a doubling premise

Most things double badly. Check these before you write, in this order. A premise that fails one is not a bad idea, it is a different film.

## The seven tests

1. **You could hold it, or do it, today.** A note, a scarf, a snooze button. Not "AI" or "the economy". It starts harmless and domestic.
2. **It has a real unit.** Thickness, length, count, mass, money or time. You will compute it, so pick one and stick to it. Say which.
3. **Someone wants something small.** Write that person's want in five words, and it cannot be about the thing doubling. "Leo wants Ivy not
   to find out." If you cannot, you have a fact and not a film.
4. **That want keeps it going.** The person causes the next step, or could stop it and does not. Folding it again hides it better. One more
   snooze is only one more snooze. If the want and the doubling do not touch, the person is a spectator.
5. **It passes four landmarks you can picture before step 50.** Do the sum first (below). Too slow and the film drags; too fast and there is no
   ladder.
6. **You can draw it in code.** A column, a pile, a sheet, a swarm, a line. Something one shape can do. Not "a smell".
7. **The last line works.** Write it now. The first person, the first want, three to seven words, no mention of the thing.

## Build the table first

This is the film's skeleton, and it takes five minutes. One row per rung:

| Step | Value | Landmark it just passed | Who is standing there | Their one line | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 14 | 1.64 m | a grown-up | the teacher, back turned | "Eyes on the board, Leo." | maths |
| 23 | 839 m | the tallest building (828 m) | nobody: a huge shot | | CTBUH |
| 42 | 439,805 km | the Moon (384,400 km) | nobody: the top | | NASA |

Rules for the table:

- Start value × 2^step. Compute it in `scenes.tsx`, not by hand, and show it as is.
- Use real, sourced landmarks, and say "about" in the sentence if the comparison is rough.
- About half the rows have nobody: those are the huge shots. The other half have a person who has just met the problem.
- The steps do not need to be even. Skip through dull ones in a single run; the readout counts them past. Slow down where a landmark or a joke is.
- Look for the true fact hiding in the maths. In any doubling, the last step alone adds as much as all the steps before it. It is true every
  time, and it lands best at the top.

## The kinds that work, by unit

These are starting points, not scripts. Do the sum. Find your own want.

| Unit | The thing | A small want that might drive it |
|---|---|---|
| Thickness | A sheet folded again and again; a stack of coupons; a sandwich | Hide it; save money; win an argument with a chef |
| Length | A scarf that doubles each night; an extension lead; a bakery queue | A gift that is nearly finished; reach the one socket; be next |
| Count | A rumour told to two friends; coat hangers in a wardrobe; relatives at a dinner | Tell only one person; find a clean shirt; seat everyone |
| Mass | A snowball; a beard (grown, not trimmed); a compost heap | Win the snowball fight; pass the job interview; please a neighbour |
| Money | A library fine; a parking fine | Refuse to pay it on principle |
| Time | The snooze button; a microwave's last minute | Five more minutes; lunch |

## What to avoid

- **Disaster for its own sake.** If everyone is screaming by step ten, there is nowhere to go. People stay calm and professional, and that is
  what is funny.
- **The narrator's irony.** The sentence is dry, never winking. No "uh oh". No "surely not".
- **A real person, brand, game or show in the premise.** Generic figures, generic towns. A landmark may be measured, and need not be drawn:
  a stepped spire the right height works better than a likeness.
- **Stopping at the top.** Without the return it is a fact with animation.
