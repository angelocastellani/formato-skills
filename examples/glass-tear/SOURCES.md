# Glass Tear: sources

Every claim in the script, with where it comes from. Checked November 2026.

| Claim in the film | Source |
|---|---|
| The head of the drop can take a hammer; the tail breaks with finger pressure | Purdue University, "Research solves centuries-old riddle of Prince Rupert's drops", 10 May 2017: the head "can withstand impact with a small hammer"; "the tail can be broken with just finger pressure". https://www.purdue.edu/newsroom/archive/releases/2017/Q2/research-solves-centuries-old-riddle-of-prince-ruperts-drops.html |
| Made by dripping molten glass into cold water | Same Purdue release: "dropping red hot blobs of molten soda-lime or flint glass into cold water". |
| On screen: 900 degrees C, glass you can shape | Wikipedia, "Soda-lime glass": readily formable at a viscosity "typically reached at a temperature around 900 C". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soda%E2%80%93lime_glass |
| The outside freezes first; the inside pulls on it as it cools | Purdue release: surface compression balanced by "compensating tensile" stresses in the interior. Wikipedia, "Prince Rupert's drop": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Rupert%27s_drop |
| On screen: the skin is about 10% of the way across | Wikipedia, "Prince Rupert's drop": the surface compressive layer is "only about 10% of the diameter of the head". |
| On screen: 400 to 700 MPa | Purdue release abstract: "large surface compressive stresses in the range of 400-700 MPa". Paper: Aben et al., Applied Physics Letters 109, 231903 (2016), DOI 10.1063/1.4971339. |
| About three elephants on a dime, at its tightest | Our arithmetic, using the top of that range. A dime is 17.91 mm across, so 2.52 cm2 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dime_(United_States_coin)). 700 MPa x 2.52 cm2 = 176 kN, about 18 tonnes-force. An adult male African bush elephant averages 6.0 t (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_bush_elephant). 18 / 6 = 3. At 400 MPa it is under two elephants, which is why the line says "at its tightest". |
| No crack gets in through the skin | Wikipedia, "Prince Rupert's drop": cracks on the surface tend to grow parallel to it and cannot enter the tension zone; a disturbance in the tail lets them in. |
| On screen: 15 mm fat end, 3 mm tail at most | Purdue release, quoting the paper's abstract: "the diameter of the head of a PRD is in the range of 5-15 mm and that of the tail is 0.5 to 3.0 mm". |
| The crack runs at about a mile a second | Purdue release: "more than 4,000 mph" (about 1.1 miles a second). Wikipedia gives 1,450 to 1,900 m/s (0.9 to 1.2 miles a second). |
| It ends as powder | Wikipedia, "Prince Rupert's drop": it disintegrates "explosively into powder". |
| A prince brought these to England in 1660 | Wikipedia, "Prince Rupert's drop": Prince Rupert "was responsible for bringing them to Britain in 1660". He did not invent them: they are recorded in Germany by 1625. The film only says he brought them. |
| A university called the riddle solved in 2017 | The Purdue release of May 2017 is titled "Research solves centuries-old riddle". The film reports that the university said so, not that nothing is left to learn. |
| Hold it by the fat end | Follows from the two facts above. A joke, and good advice. |

## What is drawn, not sourced

The brass dish, cradle, hammer, tweezers and the drawing of three gold elephants on a dime are staging. The crack is slowed down enormously for the
picture: the real thing is over in a fraction of a millisecond. The reforming dust at the end is a picture of "these", meaning more
drops, not a claim about glass. The drop is drawn far larger than life; real ones have heads of 5 to 15 mm. The cooling readout (900 down to
300 C) and the "0 cracks" counts are illustrations of the picture, not measurements; the squeeze rising through 400 to 700 MPa is the measured
range shown as a run, not a time series.
