# Writing

What holds across every format. Each format's skill adds its own engine.

## The numbers

About 2.7 words a second spoken, so 70 to 160 words for most films. Sentences average under twelve words. A long one is
followed by a very short one.

## The open

The hook is the first sentence, under two seconds to the turn. It is one of:

- **The impossible, made concrete and personal:** "What if" plus a thing you can picture, in the second person.
- **The news report:** an absurd thing stated flatly as already true, in the present tense. No "what if".
- **The cold scene:** someone says a line in the middle of it, and we work out why.

Never a question that could be answered "no, it can't". Never "did you know". Never a definition.

## The middle

- **A chain, not a list.** Each sentence is caused by the last: so, but, then. If you can swap two sentences, you have a
  list.
- **Mechanism, then surprise.** Say why it happens in plain words, then the thing nobody expects.
- **Household comparisons.** Every big number gets something you own beside it: a fridge light, a paper clip, a horse
  stable. Use the honest size of it. If it is rough, say "about".
- **One real precedent, when there is one.** A real date, a real person, a real place: it turns a game into a fact.
- **Words a nine-year-old says.** Technical words only as the punchline, named after we have seen the thing.

## The end

The last line is a button: three to seven dry words. A joke, or a small practical instruction, or the premise turned
over. Never a summary, never a moral you meant seriously, never a call to follow, comment or subscribe. The film ends on
the word.

## What to avoid

Rhetorical stacks ("not X, not Y, but Z"), "imagine", "here's the thing", "let that sink in", "the crazy part", and any
sentence whose job is to announce the next sentence. Real people are not the butt of the joke. Sad facts are not a
button.
