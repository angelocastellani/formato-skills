# The Long Summer: sources

Every claim the film makes, and where it comes from. Checked November 2026.

| Claim | Source |
|---|---|
| Arctic terns weigh about 100 g (113 g in one report, under 125 g in the tracking paper) | [National Geographic, 2010](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/article/100111-worlds-longest-migration-arctic-tern-bird); [Egevang et al., PNAS 2010](https://nora.nerc.ac.uk/id/eprint/14091/); [Wikipedia, citing the BTO: "around 100 g"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_tern) |
| A phone weighs more: an iPhone 16 is 170 g | [Apple, iPhone 16 specs](https://www.apple.com/iphone-16/specs/) |
| It breeds in the high Arctic (the tracked birds in Greenland and Iceland) in the northern summer | [Egevang et al., PNAS 2010](https://nora.nerc.ac.uk/id/eprint/14091/) |
| It stops for about a month in the open North Atlantic to feed | [National Geographic, 2010](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/article/100111-worlds-longest-migration-arctic-tern-bird) ("often stop for a month"); [National Geographic, Chasing daylight](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/chasing-daylight-tiny-trackers-reveal-the-incredible-flight-plans-of-the-arctic-tern) (3 to 4 weeks) |
| It reaches Antarctica by November, as perpetual sunlight starts there | [National Geographic, Chasing daylight](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/chasing-daylight-tiny-trackers-reveal-the-incredible-flight-plans-of-the-arctic-tern) |
| Two summers a year | [Wikipedia, citing Cramp 1985](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_tern) |
| The way home is a giant S, and they fly with the winds | [Egevang et al., PNAS 2010](https://nora.nerc.ac.uk/id/eprint/14091/) ("sigmoidal trajectory"); [National Geographic, 2010](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/article/100111-worlds-longest-migration-arctic-tern-bird) (follow spiralling wind patterns, avoid flying into the wind); [Chasing daylight](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/chasing-daylight-tiny-trackers-reveal-the-incredible-flight-plans-of-the-arctic-tern) ("an S-shape") |
| About 70,000 km a year | [National Geographic, 2010](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/article/100111-worlds-longest-migration-arctic-tern-bird) (71,000 km). The paper reports more than 80,000 km for some birds. |
| It lives "up to thirty years" | The record is 31 years; a typical lifespan is about 13. [BTO, Arctic Tern BirdFacts](https://www.bto.org/understanding-birds/birdfacts/arctic-tern). National Geographic's 30 years or more is the upper end, so the film says "up to". |
| A record-length life at about 70,000 km a year is about 2.2 million km, nearly three trips to the Moon and back | [National Geographic, 2010](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/article/100111-worlds-longest-migration-arctic-tern-bird). An estimate from lifespan, not a measurement. |

## What the picture adds

- The route on the globe is a sketch of the paper's description (south along Africa, a stop in the North Atlantic, home by
  south-west Africa, across at the equator, up the west side), not a plot of tracking data.
- The day and night follow the Sun's real declination for the month shown. The Earth to Moon figure says "not to scale".
- The wind lines are the textbook belts (trades, westerlies, polar easterlies), not a weather map.
- The coastlines are Natural Earth 1:110m land (public domain), simplified. The place names sit at their real latitude and longitude.

The hook ("never had another winter") is the premise, impossible on purpose. Nothing about any living person.
